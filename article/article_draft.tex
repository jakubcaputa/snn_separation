\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Pattern separation --- the transformation of similar input patterns into less
similar output patterns --- is the function most often attributed to the
hippocampal dentate gyrus (DG). It is also a function that is easy to assert and
hard to measure: the usual readout, a drop in pairwise correlation between
population vectors, is confounded with how sparsely the network happens to be
firing. We describe an open, data-constrained spiking model of the DG
microcircuit (granule cells, fast-spiking interneurons, hilar mossy cells)
implemented in Brian2, together with two interfaces built around it: an
interactive browser application in which a neurobiologist can move biophysical
parameters and immediately see their effect on circuit behaviour, and a headless
experimental layer that runs the same model at scale on an HPC cluster. The
model's operating point is pinned by whole-cell patch-clamp recordings
(Madar, Ewell \& Jones, 2019) rather than chosen for convenience. We present the
mechanism the model implements, the design of the application, and the
experiments carried out with it so far, including a methodological result we
consider the most transferable: decorrelation measured without a
sparsity-matched null control is not interpretable as pattern separation.

\section{Introduction}
% =============================================================================

The dentate gyrus is the entry stage of the hippocampal trisynaptic circuit and
the structure most commonly assigned the role of a \emph{pattern separator}: it
is supposed to take the overlapping cortical activity patterns arriving over the
perforant path and re-encode them into granule cell population vectors that
overlap less, so that downstream area CA3 can store them as distinct memories
without catastrophic interference \cite{marr1971,treves1994,leutgeb2007}.

Three features of DG anatomy are usually cited in support of this role. The
granule cell population is large and its activity is extremely sparse, which by
itself provides expansion recoding. Granule cells are embedded in strong
feedforward and feedback inhibition mediated by fast-spiking basket cells.
And hilar mossy cells form an excitatory loop back onto granule cells whose
functional sign has remained contested.

What is much less settled is \emph{how much} of each of these is needed, and
which of them actually carries the effect. Those questions are quantitative, and
answering them requires a model that is (i) constrained by measurements rather
than hand-tuned, (ii) cheap enough to sweep over wide parameter grids, and
(iii) transparent enough that an experimentalist --- not only the person who
wrote the code --- can interrogate it.

\paragraph{What this paper describes.} We present such a model and, more
importantly, the tooling built around it. Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} states the
mechanism and the measurement problem it poses. Section~\ref{sec:model} gives
the model and its data constraints. Section~\ref{sec:app} describes the
interactive application, which is the part intended for experimental
collaborators. Section~\ref{sec:experiments} reports the experiments run so far.
Section~\ref{sec:outlook} states what is planned and what is currently undecided.

\paragraph{What this paper does not do.} It does not advance a single
mechanistic claim about DG function. The empirical programme is still open
(Section~\ref{sec:outlook}), and we deliberately report the experiments
performed to date as demonstrations of what the framework can resolve.

\section{The mechanism and the measurement problem}
\label{sec:mechanism}
% =============================================================================

Pattern separation is defined operationally. Two input patterns are presented,
their similarity $r_{in}$ is measured, the resulting output population vectors
are recorded, their similarity $r_{out}$ is measured, and the circuit is said to
separate when
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{dec} \;=\; r_{in} - r_{out} \;>\; 0 .
  \label{eq:dec}
\end{equation}
Figure~\ref{fig:concept} shows one such measurement in the model.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig1-concept.png}
  \caption{\textbf{Pattern separation, operationally.} Two input patterns drive
  overlapping sets of granule cells (left). Each cell responds with a firing
  rate (centre). The pairwise correlation of the output vectors is lower than
  that of the inputs (right); the difference is the separation score of
  Eq.~\eqref{eq:dec}. Single run of the model at default parameters.}
  \label{fig:concept}
\end{figure}

This definition carries a trap that shapes everything in
Section~\ref{sec:experiments}. The correlation between two nearly empty vectors
tends to zero for purely statistical reasons. A network that stops firing
therefore scores a \emph{perfect} separation under Eq.~\eqref{eq:dec} while
computing nothing at all. Any manipulation that reduces activity --- which
includes every manipulation that increases inhibition --- moves the score in the
``good'' direction for a reason that has nothing to do with the circuit. We
return to this in Section~\ref{sec:nulls}, where it stops being a caveat and
becomes a result.

\subsection{Two axes of inhibition}

The model distinguishes two ways of inhibiting a granule cell, because they are
biophysically distinct and, as it turns out, behave differently:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Tonic} inhibition $\Kgc$: a standing hyperpolarising current,
        cell-intrinsic, always on, representing ambient GABA acting on
        extrasynaptic receptors.
  \item \textbf{Phasic} inhibition $\Wfsgc$: spike-driven synaptic inhibition
        delivered by fast-spiking interneurons.
\end{itemize}

These are the two principal knobs of the model (Figure~\ref{fig:circuit}), and
separating them matters: they are often lumped together as ``inhibition'', yet
only the second is part of a circuit motif.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{fig2-circuit.png}
  \caption{\textbf{The modelled microcircuit.} Perforant path (PP) input drives
  granule cells (GC) directly and fast-spiking interneurons (FS) in parallel
  (feedforward, FF); granule cells also recruit FS (feedback, FB); FS inhibit
  GC. Hilar mossy cells (HMC) form an excitatory loop. The two inhibition axes
  are indicated at lower left.}
  \label{fig:circuit}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Three dissociable motifs}

The inhibitory architecture decomposes into three motifs that can be switched on
and off independently in the model, which is what makes a formal attribution
analysis possible (Section~\ref{sec:e2}):
\textbf{FF} (PP\,$\to$\,FS\,$\to$\,GC),
\textbf{FB} (GC\,$\to$\,FS\,$\to$\,GC), and
\textbf{MC} (GC\,$\to$\,HMC\,$\to$\,GC/FS).

\section{Model and implementation}
\label{sec:model}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Neurons and synapses}

All three cell types are Izhikevich neurons \cite{izhikevich2003}, chosen as the
cheapest model that still reproduces the distinct firing signatures of granule
cells and fast-spiking interneurons. Membrane dynamics are
\begin{align}
  \frac{dv}{dt} &= 0.04\,v^2 + 5v + 140 - u + g_{ex} - g_{in} - K,
  \label{eq:izh_v}\\
  \frac{du}{dt} &= a\,(b\,v - u),
  \label{eq:izh_u}
\end{align}
with reset $v \leftarrow c$, $u \leftarrow u + d$ on each spike. Synaptic
conductances decay exponentially, $\tau_{ex}=\SI{5}{\milli\second}$ and
$\tau_{in}=\SI{8}{\milli\second}$ for granule cells. Parameters are listed in
Table~\ref{tab:params}.

The tonic term $K$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:izh_v} is what makes the two inhibition axes
separable: it enters the voltage equation directly rather than through a synapse.

\subsection{Input: the perforant path}

Each granule cell receives 40 perforant path fibres. A fibre belonging to a
driven cell fires at \SI{10}{\hertz}, a background fibre at \SI{1}{\hertz},
giving aggregate Poisson drives of \SI{400}{\hertz} and \SI{40}{\hertz}
respectively. With $W_{\mathrm{PP}\to\mathrm{GC}}=\SI{4}{\milli\volt}$ the
steady-state excitatory drive is
\begin{equation}
  g_{ex}^{ss} = r \cdot W \cdot \tau_{ex}
  = 400 \cdot 4 \cdot 0.005 = \SI{8}{\milli\volt}.
  \label{eq:gex}
\end{equation}
This single number is held as the project's frequency canon, in one file, because
an earlier audit found two different values (400 vs \SI{600}{\hertz}) in
use across scripts, which silently changed the regime: \SI{600}{\hertz} drives
granule cells to \SI{16.6}{\hertz}, far above the sparse-coding regime expected
of DG.

\subsection{Why the circuit is sparse: an analytic handle}

For the Izhikevich neuron, the resting and threshold potentials are the stable
and unstable fixed points of Eq.~\eqref{eq:izh_v}, available in closed form.
The consequence used throughout is that the drive needed to make a granule cell
fire is
\begin{equation}
  G_{crit} = 4 + K,
  \label{eq:gcrit}
\end{equation}
verified against simulation to within \SI{0.1}{\milli\volt}. At the default
$\Kgc=10$ this gives \SI{14}{\milli\volt} against an actual drive of
\SI{8}{\milli\volt} (Eq.~\eqref{eq:gex}): granule cells sit \emph{below}
threshold and fire only on fluctuations (Figure~\ref{fig:opoint}a). This is the
mechanistic basis of sparse DG coding in the model, and it is a statement one can
check rather than tune.

\subsection{Data constraints}

The operating point is anchored to whole-cell recordings from
Madar, Ewell \& Jones \cite{madar2019}, deposited as BioStudies
\texttt{S-BSST219}. Membrane properties are read from the experimenters'
own annotations rather than re-derived from the raw traces. After
de-duplication by cell identity (72 raw records resolve to 42 granule cells ---
without de-duplication the median shifts from $-76$ to $-70$\,mV), granule cell
resting potential is $-76$\,mV, IQR $[-81,-70]$.

This pins $\Kgc$ (Figure~\ref{fig:opoint}b). The model at $\Kgc=10$ rests at
$-78.7$\,mV, inside the data IQR; at $\Kgc=0$ it rests at $-70$\,mV, at the
upper edge. The default is therefore supported by the data, not merely inherited.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig3-operating-point.png}
  \caption{\textbf{The operating point is constrained.} (a) The drive required
  to spike grows as $G_{crit}=4+K$; the actual perforant path drive
  (\SI{8}{\milli\volt}) is below it at the default $\Kgc=10$, placing granule
  cells in a fluctuation-driven, sparse regime. (b) The same parameter sets the
  resting potential, which the patch-clamp data constrain independently.}
  \label{fig:opoint}
\end{figure}

\section{The application}
\label{sec:app}
% =============================================================================

The model is exposed through two interfaces that share one implementation of the
circuit. Keeping a single source of truth is a design constraint, not an
accident: an earlier version in which the interactive tool and the batch code had
drifted apart produced experimental results that no longer matched what the tool
displayed.

\subsection{Interactive tool}

\texttt{interactive\_dg.py} is a browser application (Streamlit) aimed at
experimental collaborators rather than modellers. It offers two modes: a
single-neuron mode for intuition about excitability, and a network mode running
the full microcircuit. Every biophysical parameter in Table~\ref{tab:params} is
a slider, and the following are shown live as they change:

\begin{itemize}
  \item membrane potential traces for two competing input patterns;
  \item input and output correlation matrices side by side --- the separation
        measurement of Eq.~\eqref{eq:dec} made visible;
  \item a circuit schematic whose connection widths track the actual flow of
        excitation and inhibition in the current configuration;
  \item a \emph{voltage budget} panel decomposing what excites and what inhibits
        granule cells, which is how the dominance of tonic over phasic
        inhibition first became visible;
  \item $P(\mathrm{AP}\,|\,\mathrm{pulse})$ under the stimulation protocol used
        by Madar et al., so that a model setting can be compared against the
        experiment it is supposed to match.
\end{itemize}

The intent is that a question of the form ``what happens to separation if basket
cell inhibition is halved?'' can be answered by the person who has that question,
in the browser, without reading any code.

\subsection{Headless experimental layer}

\texttt{experiments/dg\_core/} is the same circuit without the interface:
a frozen configuration dataclass, the Brian2 circuit, pattern generators,
a metric library, calibration routines, and null models. Experiments are plain
command-line scripts that support \texttt{-{}-shard i -{}-n-shards N}, so a
parameter grid maps onto a SLURM job array; results are written per shard and
merged by the analysis step. The full grids reported below run in minutes on 16
cores.

\subsection{Calibration rather than tuning}

Three quantities are set by bisection against a target rather than by hand:
the synaptic weight that achieves a required $P(\mathrm{AP}\,|\,\mathrm{pulse})$,
the phasic weight that achieves a required tonic/phasic balance, and --- added
for the experiment in Section~\ref{sec:nulls} --- the tonic inhibition $\Kgc$
that achieves a required fraction of active granule cells. The last of these
converts activity level from an outcome into a controlled variable, which is what
makes the comparison in Section~\ref{sec:nulls} meaningful.

\subsection{Reproducibility}

Default behaviour is covered by regression tests (17 tests) that assert the
circuit remains bit-for-bit identical as features are added; parameters
introduced later default to neutral values and, where relevant, take a code path
that consumes no random numbers, so earlier results need not be recomputed.

\section{Experiments performed so far}
\label{sec:experiments}
% =============================================================================

Four experiments have been run with the framework. We report them here as
demonstrations of what it resolves; the overall interpretation is deferred to
Section~\ref{sec:outlook}. Figure~\ref{fig:findings} summarises all four.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig4-findings.png}
  \caption{\textbf{Summary of experiments to date.} (a) Measured separation
  rises monotonically as the network falls silent; the apparent optimum sits at
  whatever activity cut-off the analysis imposes. (b) With activity held fixed
  by calibration, randomly shuffling which granule cell fires yields
  \emph{more} decorrelation than the real circuit. (c) Shapley attribution of
  separation to the three inhibitory motifs. (d) A linear decoder does not read
  granule cell output more accurately than it reads the raw input.}
  \label{fig:findings}
\end{figure}

\subsection{E1 --- mapping the inhibition plane}

A $10\times9$ grid over $\Kgc$ and $\Wfsgc$, five network seeds, 450 grid points
and 1800 simulations, run in \SI{3.7}{\minute} on 16 cores. The question was
whether separation has an optimum at intermediate activity.

It does not. Measured separation increases monotonically as activity falls
(Figure~\ref{fig:findings}a). Because the analysis discards points below an
activity floor --- itself a guard against the empty-vector artefact --- the
apparent maximum lands immediately above that floor, and moves when the floor
moves: cut-offs of 0.02, 0.05, 0.10 and 0.20 place the maximum at 0.025, 0.065,
0.120 and 0.200 respectively. A maximum that tracks the analyst's threshold is
a property of the analysis, not of the circuit.

\subsection{E1$'$ --- separation above a sparsity-matched null}
\label{sec:nulls}

E1 motivated the methodological core of this work. If decorrelation is partly
guaranteed by sparsity, then the quantity of interest is not decorrelation but
decorrelation \emph{in excess of} what a code of the same sparsity achieves by
chance. We therefore (i) fixed the activity level by bisection on $\Kgc$ instead
of letting it vary, and (ii) compared each measurement against two null models:

\begin{description}
  \item[Permutation null.] The output rate vector is permuted across cells,
        independently per pattern. The rate distribution is preserved exactly;
        only the correspondence between pattern and cell identity is destroyed.
  \item[Random-projection null.] A random sparse expansion of the input with the
        number of active units matched to the DG output ($k$-WTA).
\end{description}

Over 810 grid points (6 activity targets $\times$ 9 phasic weights $\times$ 3
input sparsities $\times$ 5 seeds), 733 of which could be tuned to the requested
activity, the excess over the permutation null is
$-0.389 \pm 0.245$ (SD), SEM $0.009$ --- that is, negative, and far from zero.
Shuffling which granule cell fires \emph{increases} decorrelation relative to
the intact circuit (Figure~\ref{fig:findings}b). With activity equalised, the
phasic weight $\Wfsgc$ has no detectable effect anywhere in the range $0$--$5$
(spread of means 0.024 against a per-cell SEM of 0.027).

The interpretation is mechanical rather than mysterious: which granule cells fire
is dictated by which ones receive input, so overlapping patterns drive
overlapping cells, and the circuit \emph{preserves} input correlation relative to
a random assignment.

\subsection{E2 --- attributing separation to motifs}
\label{sec:e2}

With three motifs that can be independently lesioned, the contribution of each
is well defined as a Shapley value over the $2^3$ lesion coalitions. Over a grid
of input statistics (216 points $\times$ 5 seeds), the feedforward and feedback
motifs contribute positively, while mossy cells contribute
\emph{negatively in isolation} yet show strong positive interaction with both
inhibitory motifs (Figure~\ref{fig:findings}c). Mossy cells in this model are
thus a conditional motif: on their own they re-excite granule cells and
\emph{correlate} patterns; combined with inhibition they are associated with the
largest gains.

\todo{E2 computes Shapley values on raw decorrelation, i.e. on the metric that
Section~\ref{sec:nulls} shows to be confounded. Re-run with
\texttt{separation\_vs\_null} before these numbers are claimed. The machinery
exists; the cost is minutes. Note also that the strongest apparent separation in
this grid occurs where active granule cells fire at \SI{2.2}{\hertz}, well below
the \SI{6}{\hertz} canon.}

\subsection{1A --- does a downstream decoder benefit?}

A complementary question avoids correlation metrics entirely: can a linear
classifier read pattern identity better from granule cell output than from the
raw input? Across four conditions --- raw input, DG output, DG with inhibition
removed, and a random sparse projection with matched sparsity --- it cannot
(Figure~\ref{fig:findings}d). Accuracy via DG is $0.885$ against $0.940$ for the
raw input, and the sparsity-matched random control matches DG exactly.

This is a negative result obtained with an independent measure, and it is
consistent with Section~\ref{sec:nulls}.

\section{Status and planned work}
\label{sec:outlook}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{What we take to be established}

The methodological point is the one we would most like to see adopted:
\textbf{decorrelation reported without a sparsity-matched null is not evidence of
pattern separation.} The confound is not subtle --- in our grids it is larger
than the effect --- and it is invisible to the usual guard of discarding
low-activity points, because that guard simply relocates the apparent optimum to
its own threshold. The permutation null is nearly free: it requires no additional
simulation.

\subsection{What remains open}

The framework has returned negative results for the specific separation
hypotheses tested so far. Whether the right response is to report that as the
finding, to move the question from separation to activity \emph{regulation}, or
to find a measure that sparsity cannot counterfeit, is a decision we have not
yet taken. Two candidate measures are already spent: mutual information
retention proved to be an artefact of the estimator (its optimum tracks input
sparsity rather than any circuit property), and decodability was closed by
experiment 1A.

\subsection{Planned extensions}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Adaptive control.} Whether a homeostatic rule --- intrinsic
        plasticity plus inhibitory STDP --- can find and hold the operating
        point autonomously. This is the direction we consider most likely to
        carry a positive result, because the model's behaviour is dominated by
        where its activity sits, which is precisely what such a rule regulates.
  \item \textbf{Driving the model with the experimental stimuli.} The stimulus
        protocols are recoverable from the Madar deposit; using them as perforant
        path input would move the model from ``synthetic patterns'' to ``the same
        stimuli as the experiment''.
  \item \textbf{Expansion.} Separation grows with network size at fixed
        in-degree ($+0.149 \to +0.296$ for 200 $\to$ 800 granule cells in
        preliminary runs); this should be quantified properly, and re-measured
        against the null.
  \item \textbf{Mossy cell brake.} The GC\,$\to$\,HMC\,$\to$\,GC loop is purely
        excitatory and runs away without inhibition onto mossy cells; the
        strength of that brake needs an experimental anchor.
\end{itemize}
